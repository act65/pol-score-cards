NZ Politician Scorecards is a data-driven way to assess New Zealand politicians. Every politician is scored on {{ attributes|length }} attributes, and every score links to the actual statements behind it — click any stat on a card to read the quotes, context, and the reasoning for that score.

Motivation

It bugs me that politicians can say whatever they want with little accountability for their promises, predictions, and claims. So this measures not what a politician believes, but how they conduct themselves — honestly or evasively, rigorously or carelessly, civilly or not.

That distinction is what makes any of this possible. What they believe is legitimately contested — capital gains tax, the road, the tunnel. That is what elections are for, and it can't be scored without just scoring "agrees with me". How they conduct themselves is a different kind of question. If government and legislation really are matters of reason and judgment, as Burke told the voters of Bristol in 1774, then how an MP argues isn't decoration on top of the job. It is the job. We don't send them to Wellington to hold opinions; we send them to sit through the select committee, read the officials' advice, hear the submitters, and reason their way to a decision.

The one standard we can all agree on

Take a handful of plain expectations of conduct. Get your facts right. Argue from evidence rather than fallacy. Say something, rather than something-shaped. Argue about the policy, not the other team. Attack the argument, not the person.

A good politician scores 100 on every one of them. Not "better than the other lot". Not "about average for Parliament". One hundred. Every one is a floor rather than a stretch goal, and there isn't a voter in the country who would call any of them unreasonable.

That is what makes conduct worth measuring when policy isn't. You and I might disagree completely about tax, immigration, Te Tiriti, the lot — and still agree, without argument, that the person making our case should answer the question and tell the truth while doing it.

And it is worth being blunt about why, because this isn't a preference for good manners. They are representing you. That is the whole arrangement: they carry your interests into a room you are not in, and they speak with your authority when they get there. When the MP you voted for sneers at the other side, ducks a question, or reaches for a number nobody checked, that is done in your name.

So the question isn't the comfortable one — whether politicians in general are a bit much. It is narrower, and it is aimed at you. How do you want your side to win? Is it fine for them to fight dirty, as long as they are fighting for something you believe in? Is a lie acceptable if it advances your goals?

Almost nobody, asked that directly, says yes. No party campaigns on dodging or on getting the facts wrong — every one of them claims the opposite. It is the one standard in politics that isn't up for grabs, so it ought to be possible to check. Mostly it isn't: fact-checks are one-off and forgotten inside a week, and there is no durable record of how an MP conducts themselves on the boring Tuesdays — only the clips that go viral. That is the gap this tries to fill.

How the cards work

Each stat is scored 0–100. A card's rarity (shown by its border colour: legendary, epic, rare, uncommon, common) is the politician's overall rank by the geometric mean of their {{ attributes|length }} attributes — so a single weak attribute drags the whole card down; you can't be "legendary" by being lopsided. The rarest tiers are deliberately the smallest.

The front page features politicians with enough data to be meaningful (at least {{ min_attributes }} of the {{ attributes|length }} attributes scored). You can re-order the cards by overall rarity or by any single attribute, sort high-to-low or low-to-high, and filter by party or by role. There is also a scorecard per party, aggregated over its MPs and shown against the House average.

Methodology

Every score comes from Hansard, the verbatim parliamentary record, and nothing else. The transcript of this Parliament is cut into windows of debate and each one is read by a large language model — Claude Opus 5 (claude-opus-5) — which pulls out the statements that bear on an attribute and scores each against a fixed rubric. Every score keeps the quoted statement, its date, its context and a short explanation, which is what the evidence pages show.

The rubrics are not a secret, and a score is only as good as the rubric behind it. One prompt per attribute, plain text, in the repo: attribute-extraction/prompts. The rest of the pipeline — the extractor, the resolver, the bias adjustment and the audits behind the numbers on the dataset page — is in the same repository: github.com/act65/pol-score-cards.

One source, on purpose. Hansard is the only source that is adversarial, on the record, and covers every MP equally — a party press release tells you what a party wants you to think, Hansard tells you how they behave when someone is arguing back. Measuring every MP in the same room is what makes two cards comparable. Other corpora we hold are published on the dataset page; they are not scored.

A quoted statement is checked against its own transcript before it is allowed on the site: anything that is not verbatim, is spliced together from fragments, or does not begin and end on a sentence boundary is dropped. The displayed number is a shrunk average — thin evidence is deliberately pulled toward the population middle, so a single flattering quote cannot make a card, and each attribute carries its evidence count and a confidence level for that reason.

What the ? on a card means

The six attributes do not all stand on the same ground. Civility, Rigor, Specificity and Focus are judgeable from the text alone — everything needed to score them is in the sentence.

Veracity and Divination are not. Deciding whether a claim is true, or whether a prediction came off, means going and checking a source. Those two emit a specific claim plus what would falsify it, and a separate step searches for the evidence and assigns the score. That step has not finished running. Until it does, both are shown from the model's own unaided estimate, and every one of them is marked on the card with a superscript ?, a dimmed number and an "unverified" note. Read a ? score as the model's guess, not as a fact-check — on the claims that have been checked, the guess is off by half a band or more about six times in a hundred.

Measured, but not on a card

Three attributes are measured but not shown, for three different reasons. Charisma was cut: it correlated at 0.96 with Civility, so one insult was being counted twice. Strength and Authenticity are on hold until the voting, bill and promise records they need are joined properly. Both also file a low score under the speaker even when the statement describes someone else's broken promise, which penalises MPs for the act of scrutinising opponents — that has to be fixed before either is worth showing. Forthrightness ("do they answer the question asked?") is measured but withheld: it is scored over question-and-answer pairs, and in practice only ministers answer questions in the House, so of the MPs with enough evidence to score, every single one sat on the government benches. It cannot be compared across a whole Parliament, so it is not shown.

Please read this

These scores are automated estimates, not verified fact. They are generated by an imperfect model over an incomplete sample of statements, and a politician with little coverage may be scored on very little evidence. Nothing here is an accusation of wrongdoing — always click through to the underlying statements and judge for yourself. This is a research and accountability tool, not the last word. How the instrument is checked, and what those checks do and don't establish, is on the dataset page.

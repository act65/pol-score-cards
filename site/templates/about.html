NZ Politician Scorecards is a data-driven way to assess New Zealand politicians. Every politician is scored on {{ attributes|length }} attributes, and every score links to the actual statements behind it — click any stat on a card to read the quotes, context, and the reasoning for that score.

Motivation

It bugs me that politicians can say whatever they want with little accountability for their promises, predictions, and claims. This is an attempt to make that behaviour legible: to measure not what a politician believes, but how they conduct themselves — honestly or evasively, rigorously or carelessly, civilly or not.

The {{ attributes|length }} attributes

All attributes are oriented so that higher is better, and are chosen to be politically neutral — they measure conduct, not policy positions.

How the cards work

Each stat is scored 0–100. A card's rarity (shown by its border colour: legendary, epic, rare, uncommon, common) is the politician's overall rank by the geometric mean of their {{ attributes|length }} attributes — so a single weak attribute drags the whole card down; you can't be "legendary" by being lopsided. The rarest tiers are deliberately the smallest.

The front page features politicians with enough data to be meaningful (at least {{ min_attributes }} of the {{ attributes|length }} attributes scored). You can re-order the cards by overall rarity or by any single attribute, sort high-to-low or low-to-high, and filter by party or by role. There is also a scorecard per party, aggregated over its MPs and shown against the House average.

Methodology

Every score comes from Hansard, the verbatim parliamentary record, and nothing else. The transcript of this Parliament is cut into windows of debate and each one is read by a large language model (Claude), which pulls out the statements that bear on an attribute and scores each against a fixed rubric. Every score keeps the quoted statement, its date, its context and a short explanation — which is what the evidence pages show.

One source, on purpose. Earlier versions mixed in party press releases and post-Cabinet press conferences, and that turned out to change a score more than the speaker did: the same MP scored several points higher in a press release than in the House, and the mix of sources differed sharply by party, so cross-MP comparison was not a fair fight. Hansard is the only source that is adversarial, on the record, and covers every MP equally. The other corpora are still published on the dataset page — they are just not scored here.

A quoted statement is checked against its own transcript before it is allowed on the site: anything that is not verbatim, is spliced together from fragments, or does not begin and end on a sentence boundary is dropped. The displayed number is a shrunk average — thin evidence is deliberately pulled toward the population middle, so a single flattering quote cannot make a card, and each attribute carries its evidence count and a confidence level for that reason.

How well does it work? We don't know yet.

The scoring is not currently validated. There is an evaluation harness, but the test sets it runs against turned out to be invented statements attributed to fictional politicians — American council-meeting speech, with no New Zealand speaker and not one line of Hansard in them. The accuracy figures they produce (civility r = 0.88, veracity r = 0.73, on fourteen labelled statements each) measure the model on a different task from the one we run here, so treat every accuracy figure as unvalidated. A replacement set sampled from the real corpus has been drawn but is not yet labelled. There is also only one labeller, so we have no inter-rater agreement to report.

What we can say comes from the rubrics themselves, and the six attributes do not all stand on the same ground. Civility, Rigor, Specificity and Focus are judgeable from the text alone — everything needed to score them is in the sentence.

Veracity and Divination are not. Deciding whether a claim is true, or whether a prediction came off, means going and checking a source. Those two now emit a specific claim plus what would falsify it, and a separate step searches for the evidence and assigns the score. That step has not finished running. Until it does, both are shown from the model's own unaided estimate, and every one of them is marked on the card with a superscript ?, a dimmed number and an "unverified" note. Read a ? score as the model's guess, not as a fact-check — on the claims that have been checked, the guess is off by more than half a band about six times in a hundred.

Three attributes shown in earlier versions are not here, and it is worth saying why. Charisma was cut: it correlated at 0.96 with Civility, so one insult was being counted twice. Strength and Authenticity are on hold until the voting, bill and promise records they need are joined properly — as shown before, they filed a low score under the speaker even when the statement described someone else's broken promise, which penalised MPs for scrutinising opponents. Forthrightness ("do they answer the question asked?") is measured but withheld: it is scored over question-and-answer pairs, and in practice only ministers answer questions in the House, so of the MPs with enough evidence to score, every single one sat on the government benches. It cannot be compared across a whole Parliament, so it is not shown.

Please read this

These scores are automated estimates, not verified fact. They are generated by an imperfect model over an incomplete sample of statements, and a politician with little coverage may be scored on very little evidence. Nothing here is an accusation of wrongdoing — always click through to the underlying statements and judge for yourself. This is a research and accountability tool, not the last word.

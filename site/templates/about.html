NZ Politician Scorecards is a data-driven way to assess New Zealand politicians. Every politician is scored on nine attributes, and every score links to the actual statements behind it — click any stat on a card to read the quotes, context, and the reasoning for that score.

Motivation

It bugs me that politicians can say whatever they want with little accountability for their promises, predictions, and claims. This is an attempt to make that behaviour legible: to measure not what a politician believes, but how they conduct themselves — honestly or evasively, rigorously or carelessly, civilly or not.

The nine attributes

All attributes are oriented so that higher is better, and are chosen to be politically neutral — they measure conduct, not policy positions.

Together they answer one question: when this politician talks, should I listen?

How the cards work

Each stat is scored 0–100. A card's rarity (its corner stars: legendary, epic, rare, uncommon, common) is the politician's overall rank by the geometric mean of their nine attributes — so a single weak attribute drags the whole card down; you can't be "legendary" by being lopsided. The rarest tiers are deliberately the smallest.

The front page features politicians with enough data to be meaningful (at least six of the nine attributes scored). You can re-order the cards by overall rarity or by any single attribute, and filter by party.

Methodology

We scrape public statements — party press releases, RNZ political reporting, Beehive releases, and Hansard (the verbatim parliamentary record) — and use a large language model (Claude) to find each politician's statements and score them against a rubric for every attribute. Each score keeps the quoted statement, its context, and a short explanation, which is what you see on the evidence pages.

We hold out a small labelled test set and measure how well the model's scores agree with human judgement (mean error and rank correlation) — accuracy varies by attribute. Some attributes (e.g. civility, specificity) are judgeable from the text alone and score reliably; others (e.g. veracity) require external fact-checking that the model can't do on its own, so those scores reflect plausibility and should be read with care.

Please read this

These scores are automated estimates, not verified fact. They are generated by an imperfect model over an incomplete sample of statements, and a politician with little coverage may be scored on very little evidence. Nothing here is an accusation of wrongdoing — always click through to the underlying statements and judge for yourself. This is a research and accountability tool, not the last word.

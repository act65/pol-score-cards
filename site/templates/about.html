NZ Politician Scorecards
The dataset  ·  Back to cards

NZ Politician Scorecards is a data-driven way to assess New Zealand politicians. Every politician is scored on {{ attributes|length }} attributes, and every score links to the actual statements behind it — click any stat on a card to read the quotes, context, and the reasoning for that score.

Motivation

It bugs me that politicians can say whatever they want with little accountability for their promises, predictions, and claims. This is an attempt to make that behaviour legible: to measure not what a politician believes, but how they conduct themselves — honestly or evasively, rigorously or carelessly, civilly or not.

The {{ attributes|length }} attributes

All attributes are oriented so that higher is better, and are chosen to be politically neutral — they measure conduct, not policy positions.

How the cards work

Each stat is scored 0–100. A card's rarity (shown by its border colour: legendary, epic, rare, uncommon, common) is the politician's overall rank by the geometric mean of their {{ attributes|length }} attributes — so a single weak attribute drags the whole card down; you can't be "legendary" by being lopsided. The rarest tiers are deliberately the smallest.

The front page features politicians with enough data to be meaningful (at least six of the {{ attributes|length }} attributes scored). You can re-order the cards by overall rarity or by any single attribute, and filter by party.

Methodology

We scrape public statements — party press releases, RNZ political reporting, Beehive releases, and Hansard (the verbatim parliamentary record) — and use a large language model (Claude) to find each politician's statements and score them against a rubric for every attribute. Each score keeps the quoted statement, its context, and a short explanation, which is what you see on the evidence pages.

How well does it work? We don't know yet.

The scoring is not currently validated. There is an evaluation harness, but the test sets it runs against turned out to be invented statements attributed to fictional politicians — American council-meeting speech, with no New Zealand speaker and not one line of Hansard in them. The accuracy figures they produce (civility r = 0.88, veracity r = 0.73, on fourteen labelled statements each) measure the model on a different task from the one we run here, so treat every accuracy figure as unvalidated. A replacement set sampled from the real corpus has been drawn but is not yet labelled. There is also only one labeller, so we have no inter-rater agreement to report.

What we can say comes from the rubrics themselves. Forthrightness, Civility, Rigor, Specificity and Charisma are judgeable from the text alone. Veracity, Strength and Authenticity are relations between what someone said and an external record the pipeline does not yet consult, so those scores reflect plausibility, not fact-checking. Divination is not grounded at all — it scores whether a prediction is checkable and plausible at the time of speaking, not whether it came true.

Two known defects, both affecting the live data. Strength and Authenticity currently file a low score under the speaker even when the statement is describing someone else's broken promise, so MPs are penalised for scrutinising opponents — discount both heavily. And scores differ substantially by source: a party press release scores higher on Forthrightness and lower on Civility, Rigor and Specificity than Hansard does, and the mix of sources differs by party, so cross-MP comparison is not yet a fair fight.

Please read this

These scores are automated estimates, not verified fact. They are generated by an imperfect model over an incomplete sample of statements, and a politician with little coverage may be scored on very little evidence. Nothing here is an accusation of wrongdoing — always click through to the underlying statements and judge for yourself. This is a research and accountability tool, not the last word.

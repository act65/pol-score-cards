The Card Game

A fast two-player battle played with the politician cards. Each card is a real NZ politician, and their nine attribute scores are their battle stats. The catch — and the point — is that a politician who misleads, dodges, and throws cheap shots makes a genuinely weak card: easy to hit, quick to fold, no staying power. The good cards are the ones who actually answer the question, argue in good faith, and keep their word.

(A 1-player digital prototype of this game also lives in the project; these are the rules for the physical version.)

What you need

  • A stack of politician cards.
  • A ten-sided die (d10) — or a phone dice-roller.
  • Counters (coins, beads) or pen and paper to track Health.

Reading a card

Every attribute is printed 0–100. In play, round each to the nearest ten and read the tens digit as a rating from 0 to 10 — so Veracity 78 is a rating of 8, Civility 31 is a rating of 3. All the rules below use these 0–10 ratings.

Setup

  1. Shuffle the deck. Deal each player 4 cards as their hand.
  2. Each player secretly chooses 3 to bring to the table, then reveals them together. These are your bench; the 4th stays in hand.
  3. Set each card's Health to 10 + its Strength rating and track it with counters.

Playing a round

Work through every card on the table in Initiative order (highest Divination first; break ties with a die roll). On a card's turn, it makes one attack:

  1. Aim at any one enemy card.
  2. Accuracy. Roll a d10. Roll above the attacker's Specificity rating and the attack is too vague — it misses, turn over.
  3. On target. Otherwise roll a d10 again. Roll above the attacker's Authenticity rating and the attack lands on a random enemy card instead of the one you aimed at.
  4. Damage. The hit deals the attacker's Rigor, plus the attacker's Concentration bonus (below), minus half the defender's Veracity (round down), minus 1 if the defender's owner has a Civility-8+ card on the bench. Minimum 1 damage.
  5. Riposte. The attacker then takes the defender's Forthrightness ÷ 3 in damage back.
  6. A card reduced to 0 Health is knocked out and discarded.

When every card has acted, the round ends. Players may deploy any benched-but-empty slots from hand (you can hold a maximum of three cards on the bench), then a new round begins.

Concentration

Each of your cards remembers who it attacked last turn. Attack the same enemy card again and your Concentration rises by one, adding +1 damage per stack. Aim anywhere else — or get knocked out — and it drops straight back to zero. A card can hold at most Focus ÷ 2 stacks (round down), so a Focus-9 card caps at +4 and a Focus-2 card at +1.

This is the game's one bit of long-term planning, and it is the attribute doing the work. A high-Focus politician who keeps pressing the same argument becomes genuinely dangerous by the third or fourth round. A low-Focus one lashes out at whoever is nearest and never builds anything — which is exactly what the score measures: engaging the policy, rather than swinging at the other team.

Winning

Knock out all of your opponent's politicians and you win. If both players run out of cards in the same round, the player with the most total Health remaining takes it.

Why the scores matter

Strong cards aren't the loudest politicians — they're the ones who score well across the board: durable (Strength), credible (Veracity), precise (Specificity), consistent (Authenticity), disciplined (Focus). A serial over-claimer with low Veracity has paper-thin armour; a vague one misses constantly; a bully with low Civility drags their whole team down; one who only ever attacks the other party never builds Concentration and spends the whole game hitting for chip damage. Holding a bad card and thinking "how is this person an MP?" is the game working as intended — and every number traces back to the evidence on their scorecard.

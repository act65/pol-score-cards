Forthrightness was meant to be the best attribute on the card. The question it asks is the
one everybody already shouts at the television: *was that an answer, or was it
something answer-shaped?*

It is also the easiest of the seven to score defensibly, because the evidence arrives
pre-paired. Oral questions in the House are a question and an answer, on the record, in
order. There is no need to infer what a statement was responding to. We scored **11,016
question-and-answer pairs** this way.

It is not on the card. Here is why.

## The coverage

Restricting to the 54th Parliament — the term the cards cover — and to MPs with at least
eight scored answers:

| | MPs |
|---|---:|
| Government | **31** |
| Opposition | **0** |
| **Total** | **31 of 133** |

That is the whole problem, and it is not a sampling accident. **Only ministers answer oral
questions.** An opposition MP can ask twelve questions a week for three years and never
once be scored, because Forthrightness scores the answer. A government backbencher is in
the same position. The attribute is not measuring a quality that MPs have; it is measuring
a quality that *office-holders* display.

Put that on a 133-card grid and it reads as a government bonus. Every minister gets a
seventh number; nobody else does. The scores themselves are fine. The *card* would be a
lie about what had been measured.

## Why the obvious fixes don't work

**Score the questions instead.** Asking has broad coverage — 120 MPs ask, median 67
questions each. But a question from a government backbench is usually a patsy
("What recent announcements has the Minister made about…"), and a question from the
opposition is usually an attack. Those are different acts. Scoring them on one scale
produces the same confound pointing the other way, and this time it would flatter the
opposition.

**Use written questions.** There are 180,939 of them, which sounds decisive until you look
at who asks: **62 MPs, all of them opposition.** One member alone accounts for 28,232.
Written questions are an opposition instrument; the answers are still ministers'.

**Wait for more data.** More sitting days produce more answers from the same ~31 people.
The ceiling is the size of the executive, not the size of the corpus.

## The thing we found by looking

The Q/A corpus starts on 20 July 2023, which is *before* the October 2023 election. So it
straddles a change of government, and 16 more MPs — David Parker, Chris Hipkins, Ayesha
Verrall, Carmel Sepuloni, Grant Robertson and others — carry scores from when **they** were
the ministers being asked.

That allows a comparison nothing else in this dataset allows: the same institutional role,
two different governments.

| ministers of… | mean Forthrightness |
|---|---:|
| the Labour-led government (to Oct 2023) | **66** |
| the National-led government (from Oct 2023) | **67** |

One point apart.

We had assumed, when this was set aside, that Forthrightness carried a large
government-versus-opposition gap. It does not — and the reason it appeared to is that the
comparison was never government versus opposition in the first place. It was *ministers
versus everyone else*, and everyone else was not being measured at all. Once you compare
ministers to ministers, the benches are indistinguishable.

So the honest summary is narrower and more interesting than "there is a bias": **evasion in
question time is a property of the job, not of the party holding it.** Both lots do it at
the same rate. That is a finding about Parliament rather than about any MP, which is
precisely why it belongs in a note and not on a card.

## Where it stands

Forthrightness is `WITHHELD` in the registry: still extracted, still scored, not shown.
It stays defined so the 11,016 pairs remain readable, and it is the strongest candidate for
a **ministers-only** view if we ever build one — a Cabinet scorecard, where every card on
the board is an office-holder and the comparison is fair by construction.

What it cannot be is the sixth number on a card that also belongs to a first-term
backbencher from Invercargill.

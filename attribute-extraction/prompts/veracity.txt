THE ONE QUESTION: Are the factual premises accurate?

YOU DO NOT SCORE VERACITY. This changed on 2026-08-07. Your job is to EXTRACT a
checkable claim and state what evidence would settle it. A separate resolver
then searches for that evidence and assigns the score. Leave `score` empty.

Why: the old prompt asked you to score how well-evidenced a claim *looked* and
to "prefer ~0.5" when you could not verify it. That is a judgement about
reasoning, not about fact — it is Rigor wearing a fact-check label, it made
Veracity correlate with Rigor at 0.83, and it filled the dataset with
uninformative 0.5s across 41% of the corpus. Do not reproduce it.

ELIGIBLE only when ALL of these hold:
1. The statement asserts a FACT about the world — events, data, records, what
   someone did or said. Not an opinion, not a value statement, not a promise.
2. It is a claim about the PRESENT OR PAST. Predictions belong to Divination.
3. YOU CAN STATE A FALSIFICATION CRITERION: concretely, what evidence would show
   this true, and what would show it false.

If you cannot state that criterion, the claim is not checkable and YOU MUST EMIT
NOTHING. Do not guess, do not hedge at 0.5, do not emit it with a vague
criterion. Expect to emit far fewer veracity items than you would have before —
that is the point, and it is what makes the resolver affordable.

WHAT TO EMIT
- `statement` — the claim, quoted verbatim.
- `falsification_criterion` — REQUIRED. What would settle it, specifically
  enough that someone else could go and check. Name the source if you can:
  "Stats NZ HLFS unemployment rate for the quarter stated", "the division record
  for the third reading of the Bill named". A criterion like "check if it is
  true" is useless — do not emit the claim at all if that is the best you have.
- `explanation` — what exactly is being asserted, disambiguated. NOT a verdict.
- `score` — LEAVE EMPTY. The resolver decides.
- `prior_score` — your best guess from your own knowledge ALONE, 0.0 (false) to
  1.0 (accurate), written AFTER the criterion. This is recorded so we can
  measure whether searching actually beats guessing; it is never published as
  the score. Guess honestly, not defensively: a confident 0.05 or 0.95 is more
  useful than a hedged 0.5. If you genuinely do not know, 0.5 is correct.

STATE THE CRITERION BEFORE FORMING ANY VIEW OF THE ANSWER. Fixing what would
count as proof, before looking, is the whole defence against confirmation bias.

THIS IS *NOT* VERACITY:
- Whether the CONCLUSION FOLLOWS from the claim → Rigor. An argument can be
  valid and built on false premises.
- Whether the claim is PRECISE → Specificity.
- Whether it was RUDELY expressed → Civility.

HYPERBOLE AND EXAGGERATION belong here now (they used to also count against
Civility). An overstated claim is an inaccurate claim. But only extract it if it
is checkable — rhetorical flourish that nobody could verify is not a veracity
item, it is just rhetoric.

SPOKEN LANGUAGE — READ THIS CAREFULLY. Hansard is lightly-edited live speech,
full of inversions, self-corrections and half-finished sentences. A word-order
slip is NOT a false claim. This project has already had to publicly withdraw one
example for exactly this error. If the literal reading is false but the intended
reading is obviously true from the surrounding sentences, SKIP IT.

WORKED EXAMPLES:
- "Unemployment has fallen to 3.2 percent." → EMIT. criterion: "Stats NZ
  Household Labour Force Survey unemployment rate for the quarter referenced;
  true if 3.2% ± rounding, false if materially different."
- "The Māui dolphin doesn't exist — it was a contrivance." → EMIT. criterion:
  "DOC and peer-reviewed taxonomy on whether Māui dolphin is a recognised
  subspecies of Hector's dolphin; true if not recognised, false if recognised."
- "That member voted against this bill at its second reading." → EMIT.
  criterion: "the division record for the second reading of the named bill."
- "This is the worst housing crisis in our history." → SKIP. No stateable
  criterion; "worst" is not operationalised.
- "I believe this policy is wrong." → SKIP. Opinion.
- "Inflation will fall next year." → SKIP. Prediction — that is Divination.
- "Fifty percent of Māori are in jails at the moment." → SKIP. In context this
  is a word-order slip for "fifty percent of people in jail are Māori"; the
  surrounding sentences make the intent plain. Do not score a slip as a lie.

THE ONE QUESTION: Does the conclusion follow from the premises?

Rigor is about the *thought process*, not the facts. You are asking whether the
reasoning is valid GIVEN ITS ASSUMPTIONS. You are NOT fact-checking. Whether the
assumptions are actually true is Veracity's job, and confusing the two is the
single largest defect in the previous version of this instrument.

ELIGIBLE only when the statement actually advances an argument — at minimum a
premise and a conclusion, or a claim offered with a reason. If a statement makes
a bare announcement, reports a fact, expresses a feeling, or issues a
pleasantry, there is no inference to assess. SKIP IT. Do not score it 0.5.

**A JIBE AT THE OTHER PARTY IS NOT AN ARGUMENT.** This gate is the one that
matters most, and in the first v3.0 pilot it failed: Rigor fired on
content-free attacks on opponents and reached for a fallacy label — "appeal to
authority", "circular", "non sequitur" — where the real defect was simply that
the speaker was talking about the other team instead of the policy. That is
FOCUS's job, and scoring it here too made Focus and Rigor correlate at 0.87.

So: if a statement's only failing is that it characterises opponents rather
than engaging a policy, **emit nothing for Rigor.** Focus already catches it,
and Civility catches it too if a person is attacked. Ask yourself: strip out the
part that is about the other side — is there an argument left? If not, skip.

  SKIP: "The jeering from the other side is exactly why they're disconnected."
  SKIP: "I'm sure we'll understand more about tax policy from a side with no
         business experience." (a swipe wearing an argument's clothes)
  SCORE: "It's not financially viable — I haven't seen any information saying
         otherwise." (a real inference about a real policy, and a weak one:
         arguing from absence of evidence)

The second and third look similar and are not. The third reasons about the
policy and reasons badly; the second is about the people opposite.

SCORING
- 1.0 — the conclusion is genuinely supported by what was offered.
- ~0.5 — a reasonable point, but leaning on an appeal, or with a real gap
  between what was shown and what was concluded.
- 0.0 — the conclusion does not follow: non-sequitur, strawman, false
  dichotomy, slippery slope, circular reasoning, or a bare appeal to emotion,
  popularity, tradition, authority, or anecdote.

Name the specific fallacy or appeal in the explanation.

THIS IS *NOT* RIGOR — do not let these move the score:
- Whether the premises are TRUE. That is Veracity. An argument can be perfectly
  rigorous and built on false premises: score it HIGH here.
- Whether the speaker was RUDE. That is Civility.
- Whether the statement was VAGUE. That is Specificity.
- Whether it answered the question. That is Forthrightness.
- Whether it was about the policy or the other party. That is Focus.

AD HOMINEM — a special case, and the reason Rigor and Civility used to
correlate at 0.85. An insult is a Civility failure. It is a *Rigor* failure ONLY
when the conclusion rests on it:
- "The policy fails because the member is a fool." → LOW rigor (the conclusion
  depends on the insult).
- "The member is a fool, and the policy fails because the modelling assumes
  demand the regions don't have." → rude, but the reasoning is sound. Score
  rigor HIGH. Civility will handle the insult.

WORKED BOUNDARY EXAMPLES (the ones that matter most):
- "Unemployment is at 40 percent, so the labour market is in crisis." → 0.85.
  The figure is wildly false, but IF it were true the conclusion would follow.
  High rigor, low veracity. This is the distinction; get it right.
- "Crime is up 4% per the latest stats, and the three policies the report links
  to the rise are all ones this government cut." → 0.85 (evidence offered, the
  conclusion tracks it).
- "If we let them ban this, soon they'll ban everything." → 0.1 (slippery
  slope; nothing offered to connect the two).
- "Everyone I talk to agrees with me, so the policy must be right." → 0.15
  (appeal to popularity and anecdote).
- "The Minister opposed this in 2019 and supports it now." → SKIP. That is an
  observation, not an argument. No inference to assess.
